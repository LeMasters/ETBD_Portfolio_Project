# Five More Guidelines: The Hiring-Side View

A companion set, drawn from what studios, recruiters, and art directors say publicly about how they actually review junior portfolios. These are deliberately *non-overlapping* with the first five: those concern what a portfolio must communicate, these concern how portfolios get filtered.

---

## 1. Declare a discipline; make it legible in the first five seconds

Studios have consolidated around specialists. Reviewers scan first for a match between a job title and what the page appears to be — environment artist, systems designer, gameplay programmer, tech artist, UI. A page that shows a little of everything reads as *undecided*, and undecided is the easiest kind of candidate to skip. This is uncomfortable advice for students trained in an interdisciplinary program, where breadth is the point of the degree. Breadth still helps; it just cannot be the front door.

**Do:** Put a discipline claim in your header — "Level Designer," not "Creative." Give it the top of the page and the majority of the work. Range goes into a clearly subordinate section, or into a second page built for a different target.

**15-second check:** Could a recruiter drop your page into a pile labeled with one job title without hesitating?

---

## 2. Show the process, not only the product

The finished asset proves you can finish. The iteration proves you can *think* — and thinking is what a junior is actually hired for, since your first-year output will be rebuilt anyway. Blockout to grey-box to final. Playtest note to design change. First pass to third pass, side by side. Design and level design portfolios in particular are expected to carry documentation: flowcharts, beat charts, top-down layouts, spreadsheets, before-and-after comparisons.

**Do:** For at least two projects, show a decision: what the problem was, what you tried, what the evidence said, what changed. Screenshot your process *while working* — it cannot be reconstructed later.

**15-second check:** Does any piece on your site show a version that isn't the final one?

---

## 3. You are judged by your weakest piece, not your average

Reviewers do not compute a mean. A single mediocre or unfinished item establishes your floor, and the floor is what a hiring decision is underwritten by — nobody wants to explain a bad hire by pointing at the good pieces. This is why "add more work" is almost always the wrong instinct: additional pieces can only lower the floor or leave it unchanged.

**Do:** Rank your pieces honestly, then delete everything below the median. Repeat until deleting hurts. Unfinished work comes out entirely — a WIP labeled WIP is still a WIP.

**15-second check:** If a reviewer saw only your worst remaining piece, would you still want the interview?

---

## 4. Be exact about authorship

Almost everything an undergraduate shows is collaborative, derived, or assisted, and reviewers assume this. What they penalize is ambiguity, because ambiguity has to be resolved in the interview and often isn't survivable there. Say which parts of the team project were yours. Say when a project follows a tutorial, uses a marketplace asset pack or a template, or involves generative tools, and say specifically where. Precision here reads as professionalism, not as apology; vagueness reads as a claim you can't defend.

**Do:** Add a one-line credit under each collaborative or derived piece: your role, your scope, and what came from elsewhere. Third-party assets, engines, templates, and AI assistance each get named.

**15-second check:** For every piece, could you survive the question "walk me through what *you* did here"?

---

## 5. Design for the next step, not for the end of the visit

A portfolio's job is finished only when someone contacts you or walks into a meeting holding a question about your work. So: contact information on every screen, not only a contact page. A downloadable, current resume. Working links, including the ones to your own build. An explicit availability line — students routinely omit that they're graduating in May and looking. And at least one piece interesting enough to be *asked about*, since the interview is largely a conversation about your portfolio.

**Do:** Audit for dead ends — broken videos, private links, a mailto that nobody clicks. Add one line stating what you're looking for and when you're available.

**15-second check:** From any page, how many clicks to your email address and your resume?

---

## Points of tension with the first set

Worth naming rather than smoothing over:

- **Specialization vs. audience.** Guideline 1 here says pick one discipline; the audience principle in the first set says tailor to the reader. These agree only if the student has *one* target. Students canvassing several kinds of employer may need more than one front page — which is more work than most will do, and worth saying out loud.
- **Process vs. economy.** Showing iteration adds material; cutting to the floor removes it. The reconciliation is that process belongs in *depth* (a few projects shown thoroughly) rather than *breadth* (many projects shown shallowly).
- **Hiding the coursework.** Some industry guidance advises downplaying the academic origin of student projects; recruiter-side accounts complain that juniors show *only* class assignments. The reconcilable version: keep the assignment prompt out of the frame, but let self-directed work carry the personality. Whether to advise students to obscure that work is coursework is a judgment call — and a defensible one either way.
