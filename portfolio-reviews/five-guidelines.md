# Prototype Guidelines for ETBD Games Portfolio (INTERNAL)

A portfolio is not a storage container for your work. It is an argument about you, made out of your work. These five guidelines organize what we look for in review — and what you should look for before you send it to us.

---

## 1. Build for a specific reader, not for everyone

You cannot curate until you know who is looking. A small indie studio hiring a generalist, a mobile F2P shop hiring a UI specialist, and an ad agency hiring a motion designer want three different pages from you. A portfolio built for "anyone" persuades no one.

**Do:** Name your target reader in one sentence before you choose a single asset. Let that sentence decide what goes in, what order it appears in, and what vocabulary you use.

**15-second check:** Can someone else read your landing page and tell you what job you want?

---

## 2. Narrate every piece — never let the work "speak for itself"

You have looked at your protagonist's jump animation for three hundred hours. Your reviewer has never seen it. Every screencap, clip, and character sheet needs a few words answering two questions: *What am I seeing?* and *Why are you showing it to me?* Name your role, your tools, and the problem you solved. "Rigged for console; Maya" tells a reviewer more than a beautiful unlabeled loop.

**Do:** Caption everything. One or two lines: role, tool, problem solved, outcome. Subtitle or caption your videos.

**15-second check:** Pick any image at random. Without scrolling, can a stranger say what you did and what it demonstrates?

---

## 3. Cut until every item earns its place

Reviewers are busy and skimming. Volume reads as indecision; selection reads as judgment. Front-load your strongest and most recent work, and push the rest into a clearly labeled "Older Work" section — or out entirely. Three pieces that make an argument beat fifteen that make a pile.

**Do:** For each item, ask what work it does *that nothing else on the page already does*. If you can't answer, cut it.

**15-second check:** Is your best piece visible without scrolling?

---

## 4. Make it load, work, and identify you everywhere

The most common portfolio failures are mechanical, not aesthetic. Your name must sit at the top of every screen — not only in the URL, not only in the browser tab. Assume mobile: hiring managers read portfolios in bed as often as at a desk. Assume a slow connection and an old browser. Avoid depending on JavaScript for anything essential, and don't promise a link you haven't built ("BLOG" that leads to one post).

**Do:** Test on every device you can borrow — a parent's outdated browser, a friend's off-brand phone. Keep video headers light. Point every link at *your* account of your contribution, not a Wikipedia page that sends the reader away.

**15-second check:** On a phone, on page three, can a reader see your name and know whose site this is?

---

## 5. Decide what the portfolio claims about you — then break rules on purpose

Every choice you make argues something: that you are meticulous, or flexible, or funny, or a collaborator, or an auteur. Make that argument deliberately rather than by accident. The guidelines above are defaults, not laws — an unconventional portfolio can be exactly right, provided you can say what you gained by departing. What you cannot afford is a departure you didn't notice making.

**Do:** Write one sentence — for yourself, not for the page — finishing "This portfolio should convince a reviewer that I am ___." Then check whether the page delivers it.

**15-second check:** Name the one quality your portfolio is built to demonstrate. Does the evidence support it?

---

### A note on scope

Portfolios are long-term projects, and there is a point past which fiddling with color schemes stops paying. Take our comments as advice, not verdicts; where you have a clear reason for a choice, keep it. Where we think something needs serious rethinking, we will say so plainly.
