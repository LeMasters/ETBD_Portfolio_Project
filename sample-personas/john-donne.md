### Why "background artist", not "environment artist" (a faculty note to keep)
In job listings the two titles point to different work:
- **Background artist / background painter:** 2D painted environments, in animation and in 2D or illustrated games. This is John's job.
- **Environment artist:** in 3D games this usually means modeling, texturing and lighting in-engine. It is a different job.

John paints *environments*, but his title is *background artist*. Putting that in his first line keeps recruiters for 3D roles from misreading him. The remaining choice is which market he addresses first, **games or animation** (Lever A vs B in §7).

## 4.1 *Workshop of the World*: eight painted backgrounds of industrial Philadelphia, 2025–present  ★ flagship (case study)
- **Idea:** "Workshop of the World" was Philadelphia's old nickname. Eight 2D backgrounds for an imagined side-scrolling exploration game, set along the Delaware riverfront and the El: rowhouse roofs, a shuttered textile mill, a refinery at dusk, an empty El platform. (The title is also an existing board game's; see §11.)
- **Style:** Precisionism meets flat Modern colour. Sheeler's clean industrial planes, de Chirico's long shadows and empty squares, Matisse-like colour blocks for the skies.
- **Process (the case study sections):**
  - photo walks in Philadelphia and Oxford
  - 30 value thumbnails per scene
  - a **colour script** for the whole set: one strip showing how the light moves from morning to night across the eight scenes
  - line layout over a rough perspective guide (a quick Blender blockout only where the perspective is complex)
  - final paint in Photoshop, separated into foreground, playfield and parallax layers, with the walkable line and the character's scale marked
  - a playable side-scrolling demo in Godot, so the backgrounds are walked through, not just looked at
- **The sign that slid** (added 23 Nov, end of the Layout and paint section): he caught one mistake only when he walked the El platform in the demo. The station name slid off its sign as the camera moved: he'd put the letters on the skyline layer. Moving them onto the platform kept the sign bolted together. It is the case study's plainest argument for building the demo.
- **Numbers:**
  - 8 backgrounds at 3840 × 2160, each 25–35 hours (his time log)
  - demo played by ~60 people at the ETBD Expo, Spring 2026 [VERIFY event name]; source: his tally sheet
  - one eye-tracking pilot (5 participants, run in the lab with permission) showed players missed the exit door in Scene 3; he repainted its value contrast, and the before/after is a section of the case study
- **Honest limitation:** the scenes are beautiful and nearly empty of props. He is working on "lived-in" clutter, which Modernism taught him to leave out.

**Scene 3, in John's voice:** "I gave the mill windows the lightest value in the painting. Then I wondered why people were looking up there when the way out was at street level." For the comparison, keep the crop and the character's position identical. In the repaint, the windows sit closer in value to the brick and the light catches the edge of the exit door. **The overcorrection** (added 23 Nov): his first correction lit up the entire door, and at game scale it looked pasted on. In the repaint the door is almost as dark as the wall; all he kept was the blade of daylight along the jamb. The pilot prompted the repaint. A second test is still an open question.
